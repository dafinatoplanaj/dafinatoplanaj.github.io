The book Calm - The School of Life opens with the chapter Romantic Relationship and few days ago I read those very well written blog series by Henrik Karlsson about love and I thought a lot about some parts of it while reading this chapter. The term ‘romantic’ gets challenged in my mind while reading, and I tried to find comfort from Henrik but somehow those both pieces meet.

The author, whoever wrote this book speaks a lot about our expectations in love. Apparently, expectation is kind of our enemy and the advice “lower down your expectations” isn’t that bad after all.

It starts by sitting with the uncomfortable idea that before we even meet someone we’ve already been dreaming about how the other person should be and have this whole idealisation of how they should fullfill us. When we finally meet them based on those dreams we will create expectations and then based on them we will generate how things should go. 

*We assume that we know everything about the significant other and they know everything about us.* It seems like expectations leave no space for freedom, exploration, adventure and we are imprisoned in our minds trying to make everything seem ‘right’. And when it doesn’t then we blame this other person. 

‘We don’t blame love’ - the book says. Because love is perfect, and therefore we look for a new partner. We might have gotten disappointed several times from dating but we still keep our expectations. How do we let that go?

Believe it or not, pessimism could be the answer. We are used to look down on this word because it sounds like having a grey cloud on top of us all the time, and in love it might seem as the enemy of affection. *But actually in love it is the ‘hope’ that endangers everything that we might wish for.* 

It suggests to enter a relationship by expecting to be misunderstood. We are still dealing with an expectation but in another form. See it as a divergence arc instead of the convergence one —starting to feel more understood at the beginning to small misunderstandings showing up with time. 

If we just think about consciousness itself, the researcher Giulio Tononi, suggests that even two people going through the exact same thing at the same time don't experience it identically — each conscious mind integrates the moment in its own way. I believe that there is no such thing as being ‘similar’, yet we are convinced that we need similarity in order to create strong relationships. *It’s worth remembering that our lovers are separate beings, therefore they have contrary perspectives at times.* 

More we have in common with someone, easier we might fall in love with them because we feel less lonely. Then two people grow apart, create differences, they don’t know how to follow each other and love fades away with it too. 

The book suggests being more of a classic than a romantic. A classic person learns how to deal with a relationship just like they learn about other things in life, accepts that there will be no-go areas, that there will be zones of privacy and resignation. Whereas a romantic is more of ‘no one learns how to be something’, and that there is no convention constraining what they could or couldn’t say. 

*A classic way of being might help us to manage tension and lower hopes about what good relationships are like*. It is in this part that I thought a lot about series of Henrik’s blog which is called “Looking for Alice”.

I’m not someone to judge if Gertrude Stein (the one that likes Alice) is a classic or a romantic but one thing for sure is that she didn’t categorize Alice, she was kind of more into individuals. When Stein was asked if she was lesbian, she responded *‘No, I just like Alice’*. She didn’t have a type, and having a type would mean it’s that expectation we have already created about someone before we even get to know them. 

In the part 5 called *Love is about being invested in someone’s continual expansion,* Henrik opens up about few struggles when it comes to love or his lover. For Henrik, his wife is his Alice but how did he manage to not let Alice go for all this time? 

His wife found a new version of herself when she met Henrik. She grew and changed beyond recognition as he says, and sometimes that growth can be the wrong direction and speed for someone that risks misalignment. For Henrik *change answered change*, as a form of loop. That loop once fell apart. It was during that time that he read an essay about being a dad when he gets reminded about the unconditional parent-child love and the fact that the love you feel for your child has little to do with who they are or what they do. 

He argues about the fact that adult love is not of that form. It isn’t fully unconditional. What he learnt is that to *love someone means to extend your care to the things your partner cares about to some degree, and learning to feel what the other person is feeling*. He became an aspirant, and to aspire he says, means *to want something before you actually want it*. He learnt to see through her with her in order to meet her wherever she was in that moment.

To me it seems like Henrik is a mix of both. On one side he communicated like a romantic and on the other side he listens like a classic. Maybe that’s the point. Not to choose one or another, not to see things as either black or white but to know when to reach and when to accept.
